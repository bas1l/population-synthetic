The C2ST, hands-on: an interactive walkthrough

This is the interactive companion to the C2ST deep-dive note. It rebuilds the same four steps — one-hot encode → balance the classes → cross-validated Mann–Whitney AUC → permutation p-value — but here you drive them. Everything on this page is one linked pipeline: the master synthetic divergence knob at the top and the class counts in Step 2 flow downward, so a change in one step ripples through the AUC and p-value below. Nothing is sent anywhere; the maths matches the code in multivariate.py:c2st.

Scenario — synthetic divergence 0.00

The one master knob: how far the synthetic population's marginals drift from the real ones. 0 = a perfect twin; higher = more different. Every step below reacts to it — the profile's score in Step 1, the population and ROC in Step 3, the p-value and verdict in Step 4.

twin 0.0 very different 1.0

STEP 1Encode a whole profile (one-hot) → a score

A classifier needs numbers, not labels. Each attribute with N categories becomes an N-cell block; the person's value lights exactly one cell per block. That vector collapses to a single score — "how synthetic does this profile look?" — via the log-odds Σ log(p_syn/p_real), evaluated at the divergence above. The table below shows the arithmetic: for each category you pick, p_real is its share of the real population and p_syn its share of the synthetic one, and their log ratio is that attribute's contribution to the score (equal shares → log 1 = 0). That score is exactly what Step 3 ranks. Set employment to student — a value the scheme doesn't know — and its whole block goes all-zero ("other"), contributing nothing.

STEP 2Balance the classes

Real usually vastly outnumbers synthetic, and a classifier can score high just by always guessing "real". So both classes are subsampled to balanced_n = min(n_real, n_syn). This number sets how many people Steps 3 & 4 actually work with — drag the sliders and watch the live population downstream grow or shrink. Push the synthetic count below 2 to see the degenerate NaN case the code guards against.

↓  balanced_n per class flows into the population below  ↓

STEP 3Scores → Mann–Whitney AUC

Cross-validation gives one out-of-fold score per person. The AUC is a pure ranking statistic on those scores: the chance a random synthetic person outranks a random real one.

A · One person → one score

Here is the most synthetic-looking individual in your current population, and exactly how their score is built: sum the per-attribute log-odds log(p_syn / p_real) over the four categories they land in. A category that is more common in synthetic than in real adds a positive term (looks synthetic); rarer adds a negative one. At divergence 0 every ratio is 1, every term is log 1 = 0, so the score is 0.

B · The rank identity — a worked example you can drive

Three real (teal) and three synthetic (orange) people. Drag a chip left/right (or focus it and use ← →) to change its score; the worked calculation under it re-runs live. Or press Load 6 from your population to pull six real people sampled at the current divergence, so this little example uses the very same data as the population below. Line every chip up equal and the AUC snaps to exactly 0.500.

C · The whole population — the identical formula, at scale

Now 150 real + 150 synthetic people (that count comes straight from balanced_n in Step 2, capped at 300 for a snappy demo). Each person's score is the log-odds from part A; the same rank formula from part B, applied to all of them, is the AUC. At divergence 0 the two are identical → AUC is exactly 0.5 and the ROC hugs the diagonal; turn the master knob up and the curve bows toward the top-left.

↓  the population's observed AUC is what Step 4 tests  ↓

STEP 4The permutation p-value & the verdict

An AUC just above 0.5 could be noise. Shuffle the real/synthetic labels many times — destroying any real difference — and recompute the AUC each time. The observed AUC (from Step 3B) is significant only if few shuffles beat it. p = (1 + #{perm ≥ observed}) / (1 + n_perm), floored at ≈ 1/201. A high p is good. Move the master knob, then re-run to watch the verdict flip.

Run the shuffle to see the verdict.

Once the classifier genuinely cannot beat a coin flip — AUC ≈ 0.5 with a high p-value — the synthetic population is statistically indistinguishable from the real one on the joint profile. That is the single strongest fidelity signal in the stack.
